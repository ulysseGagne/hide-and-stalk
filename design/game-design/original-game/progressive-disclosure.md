===============================================================================
JET LAG: HIDE + SEEK  --  HOW PROGRESSIVE DISCLOSURE MAKES THE GAME
FEEL SIMPLE AND FAST IN THE FIRST FEW MINUTES OF EACH EPISODE
===============================================================================

This document is the analysis called for by step 2 of prompt.txt. It is based
on the three transcripts (Japan, Switzerland, NYC) and describes the TECHNIQUE
the show uses to teach a genuinely complex game (62-80 questions, ~40 distinct
cards, an economy, an endgame) without ever stopping the action to read a
rulebook. The findings feed directly into how rules.txt is structured and into
how the CIA club should introduce the game on activity day (see rules.txt,
section 12 "Teaching the game on the day").

Jet Lag hide-and-seek is a big ruleset. If you dumped all of it on a new player
(or viewer) up front, it would feel like homework and the pace would die. The
show never does that. Instead it reveals exactly one mechanic at the moment it
first becomes relevant, and not a second before. That single design choice is
what makes the opening minutes feel simple and fast. Below is how it works.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE ONE-SENTENCE GOAL COMES FIRST, ALONE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every episode opens by stating the objective in a single sentence before any
mechanic is mentioned:

   "The goal of this game is simple: Hide for as long as you can without
    being found."  (Japan)
   "The goal is simple: Hide for as long as we can without being found."
    (Switzerland)

The word "simple" is doing deliberate work. The viewer is handed a complete,
correct mental model of the WIN CONDITION in one breath. Everything that comes
after is understood as "detail in service of that one goal," not as new goals.

Note also what is NOT said yet: nothing about cards, curses, coins, categories,
the endgame, rest periods, or scoring formulas. Those do not exist for the
viewer yet.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. A COMPACT "FRAME" OF ~7 FACTS, THEN AN EXPLICIT PROMISE TO DEFER THE REST
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Immediately after the goal, the narrator lays out a tight frame -- roughly
seven facts, each one sentence -- that is enough to start playing and nothing
more:

   - There are Seekers and a Hider.
   - The Hider got a head start (3.5 h in Japan) to reach a hiding spot.
   - Seekers find them by asking questions from a big menu (62 / 76 / 80).
   - The Hider must answer truthfully.
   - In exchange for answering, the Hider draws cards (advantages) from the
     Hider Deck -- Time Bonuses and Curses.
   - When the Hider is found, roles rotate.
   - After N days, the longest single hiding run wins.

Then the crucial move -- an explicit deferral marker:

   "We'll go over all the nitty gritty rules later. For now, [the] clock is
    ticking."  (Japan)
   "We'll get more into the other rules once they're relevant."  (Switzerland)
   "For those of you who don't know how our hide-and-seek game works, here's
    the gist."  (NYC)

"Here's the gist" and "we'll cover the rest later" give the viewer PERMISSION
not to understand everything yet. This removes the anxiety that normally makes
rules explanations feel slow. The frame is a promise: you have enough to follow
along; trust us with the timing of the rest.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE QUESTION CATEGORIES ARE TAUGHT AS "SIX SIMPLE TOOLS", NOT A LIST
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The single most complex part of the game -- the 62-question menu -- is NOT read
out. Instead the menu is compressed into six named tools, each with a one-line
verb and a concrete local example (Japan):

   - Matching   -> "is X the same or different from ours?"
                   (his prefecture? his nearest major city?)
   - Measuring  -> "is he closer to or further from X than we are?"
                   (closer to a Shinkansen line? a coastline?)
   - Thermometer-> "after we travel a set distance, are we hotter or colder?"
   - Radar      -> "is he within a certain distance of us, yes/no?"
   - Photo      -> "send us a picture of X" (a bridge, the tallest mountain)
   - Tentacles  -> deferred: "a new tool for dealing with dense metro areas
                   that we'll get into once it's relevant."

Why this is fast:
   (a) CHUNKING. 62 questions become 6 memorable buckets. Human working memory
       holds ~6-7 chunks; the design lands exactly on that limit.
   (b) A VERB PER TOOL. Each category is defined by what it DOES for the seeker
       (same/different, closer/further, hotter/colder, within-range, show-me,
       nearest-which), so the viewer learns a capability, not vocabulary.
   (c) CONCRETE-FIRST. Every abstract tool is immediately paired with a local
       instance ("is his prefecture the same as ours?"). The viewer never holds
       an abstraction without an example attached.
   (d) ONE TOOL IS OPENLY WITHHELD. Tentacles is named but explicitly parked
       "until it's relevant," which both shortens the intro AND teaches the
       viewer the show's core method: tools arrive when they're used.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. JUST-IN-TIME REVEAL: EACH MECHANIC IS EXPLAINED THE FIRST TIME IT FIRES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the heart of the technique. The deeper rules are never front-loaded;
each one is explained in a short aside at the exact moment a player first
triggers it. Traced through the Japan episode in order of appearance:

   - CARD-DRAW REWARD is explained the first time a question is answered:
     "A Radar is worth two cards. So I get to look at the top two cards of the
      deck and draw one of them into my hand."  (Different question types are
     later shown to be worth different draws -- Thermometer = look at 2 draw 1,
     Photo = draw 1 blind, Matching = look at 3 draw 1 -- each stated the first
     time THAT type is answered.)

   - HOW CURSES WORK is explained the first time a Curse is drawn:
     "So, just really quick, a note on how Curses work. These are cards that
      [the Hider] can keep in his hand and play against us..."

   - CASTING COST is explained in the same breath, because the first two curses
     drawn happen to carry costs:
     "...they also both have casting costs that level the playing field. The
      first Curse makes our next question free, and the second Curse could give
      away valuable information, because the place [he] sends us needs to be
      further from him..."

   - PHOTO ANSWER TIME is stated the first time a Photo is requested:
     "It takes 15 minutes for him to answer."

   - The ENDGAME is announced only when it starts: "they've arrived. And the
     endgame has begun."

   - REST PERIODS, the QUARTER-MILE HIDING ZONE, the LEMON-FALLS-OFF PENALTY,
     the DICE-TO-ENTER mechanic of Jammed Door, etc. -- each surfaces the first
     time it matters in play, never before.

In Switzerland the same pattern governs the money system. Coins accumulate
silently for a while; only when the Hider has enough to act does the narrator
say: "Alright, let's finally talk about what these coins are for." The rule
(coins -> roll curse dice; 50 coins per die; higher roll = better curse) is
taught at the exact moment it becomes actionable.

Effect on pace: the intro can be short because ~70% of the ruleset is offloaded
to later "teachable moments." The viewer is never asked to hold a rule in
memory before seeing it used, so nothing feels abstract or slow.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. LEARN-BY-WATCHING-ONE-TURN: THE FIRST PLAY IS THE TUTORIAL
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The opening is structured as a single fully narrated turn that happens to
exercise the core loop end to end:

   pick a strategy -> choose a question -> ask it -> get the answer ->
   mark the map -> (Hider draws cards) -> react.

By watching one real turn, the viewer absorbs the loop procedurally instead of
declaratively. The seekers voice their reasoning out loud ("the one-two punch
we want is: confirm he's not in the metro area, then get a direction"), which
teaches not just the rules but how to THINK inside them -- turning a rules
explanation into a strategy demonstration, which is inherently more engaging.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. DUAL-PERSPECTIVE REINFORCEMENT (SEEKER SIDE + HIDER SIDE)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every mechanic is shown from both ends within a minute of each other. When the
seekers ask a Radar, we cut to the Hider: "A Radar is worth two cards, so I get
to look at the top two and draw one." When the seekers get cursed, we see the
Hider choose and cast that curse and pay its cost. The same rule is thus
presented twice, from two motivations, so it lands without a repeat explanation.
This is spaced repetition disguised as editing.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. URGENCY FRAMING KEEPS THE INTRO FROM EVER STALLING
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The clock is invoked precisely to cut the rules explanation short:
   "We'll go over the nitty gritty later. For now, [the] clock is ticking."
   "Right now, [the] clock is ticking. So [we] need to figure out what to ask."

The ticking-timer frame does two things at once: it justifies deferring rules
("no time to explain, we have to move"), and it converts the tutorial into a
race. The viewer feels momentum during what is, structurally, a rules lesson.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. PROGRESSIVE COARsening -> REFINING OF DECISIONS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The first questions shown are deliberately the COARSEST possible (eliminate an
entire metro area with a 100-mile Radar; get a cardinal direction with one
Thermometer). Fine tools (quarter-mile Radar, Tentacles, precision photos) are
demonstrated only later, in the endgame. So the viewer's understanding scales
with the map: big simple cuts first, precise tools once the search area is
small. Difficulty is disclosed in step with need.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. SUMMARY: THE DISCLOSURE LADDER
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

   Second 0     : one-sentence WIN CONDITION.
   Seconds 1-30 : ~7-fact FRAME + explicit "we'll explain the rest later."
   Minute 1     : 6 question categories as one-line tools w/ local examples
                  (one tool, Tentacles, openly deferred).
   Minute 1+    : the loop demonstrated once, live, with reasoning narrated.
   As-needed    : card draws, curses, casting costs, answer times, endgame,
                  rest periods, penalties -- each revealed the first time it
                  fires, from both Seeker and Hider perspectives.
   Endgame      : precision tools introduced only once the area is small.

The takeaway for the CIA club: DO NOT read the rulebook aloud on activity day.
Teach the one-sentence goal, the six tool-categories, and the core loop -- then
let curses, casting costs, and the endgame be explained the first time each one
actually happens in play. A 3-minute intro plus a "we'll explain cards when you
draw your first one" is faithful to the show and gets people moving fast.
See rules.txt section 12 for the scripted version of this intro.
